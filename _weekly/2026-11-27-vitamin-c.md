<h2>One micronutrient, one day</h2>
<p>Vitamin C is <strong>L-ascorbic acid</strong>, a water-soluble organic molecule — a vitamin in the strict sense, unlike the mineral magnesium in the previous chapter. Humans, unlike most animals, cannot synthesise it endogenously, so it has to arrive with food. That one fact is why a molecule this ordinary once decided the fate of sailors.</p>

<h2>What the sources actually say</h2>
<p>NIH ODS (Health Professional Fact Sheet, checked 27 Nov 2026) describes vitamin C as required for the biosynthesis of collagen, L-carnitine and certain neurotransmitters, and as a physiological antioxidant that can regenerate vitamin E. The same fact sheet says it improves absorption of nonheme iron — the form found in plant foods — and that insufficient intake causes scurvy. It also reports a pharmacokinetic detail worth quoting rather than interpreting: roughly 70–90% is absorbed at intakes of 30–180 mg/day, but absorption falls below 50% above 1 g/day, with the unmetabolised fraction excreted in urine.</p>
<p>NIH lists adult RDAs of 90 mg/day for men and 75 mg/day for women, plus 35 mg/day for people who smoke. Those are the source's reference values, not a target we set for anyone. EFSA's 2013 opinion (EFSA Journal 2013;11(11):3418) gets its numbers another way: an Average Requirement of 90 mg/day and a Population Reference Intake of 110 mg/day for men, and 80/95 mg/day for women, anchored on balancing metabolic losses at a fasting plasma ascorbate near 50 µmol/L. Two authorities, two methods, different numbers — which is exactly why we quote and never prescribe.</p>

<h2>What we will not say</h2>
<p>No “take X milligrams”, no blood-value target presented as a personal instruction, no promise about colds, cancer, cardiovascular risk, skin or wound healing. Reference values appear here only as attributed citations with a date and a link. This is an evidence note, not a recommendation — and not a place to buy anything.</p>
